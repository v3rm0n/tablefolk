How peer-to-peer Sasku works

There is no game server holding the deck. Four browsers use Nostr relays to establish WebRTC links, then jointly encrypt and shuffle the 36 cards, deal private hands, and verify every play against a signed transcript.

Connecting the table

A public relay introduces the four browsers. Once direct links open, all game traffic stays between the players.

  1. Step 1

    Invitation link

    The URL fragment carries the 16-byte game ID, the host’s Ed25519 public key, and the rules profile. Because it sits after #, the fragment is never sent in HTTP requests.

  2. Step 2

    Relay introduction

    Browsers exchange room-encrypted WebRTC offers and ICE network candidates over Nostr relays. Relays only pass connection setup messages, never game moves.

  3. Step 3

    Authenticated mesh

    Every pair opens a direct WebRTC data channel and signs both DTLS certificate fingerprints. The host then signs the four-seat roster, and all four players sign readiness.

Connection messages

Each peer verifies identity and catches up on saved history before any card is dealt.

g · h · r · s Invitation
Encodes the game ID, host Ed25519 public key, rules profile, and signaling adapter in the URL hash.
SDP / ICE Signaling
WebRTC session descriptions and candidates encrypted with a room-derived AES-GCM key. STUN finds reachable addresses; TURN is not configured.
HELLO Channel auth
Binds each player’s Ed25519 identity to the game ID and both observed DTLS certificate fingerprints before unlocking game traffic.
ROSTER / READY Agreement
The host signs the four-seat order and rules hash. Every seat signs readiness for that exact hash.
seq / prev Transcript chain
Every record is canonical CBOR signed with Ed25519, chained by sequence number and previous hash. Browsers replay and validate the exact bytes on reload.

Playing a hand

The deal rotates each round. Every browser holds its own secret key share and checks every proof locally.

  1. Step 1

    Joint key & shuffle

    All four seats contribute public key shares to form one ElGamal deck key. Each player permutes and re-masks the 36 cards in turn, attaching a zero-knowledge shuffle proof.

  2. Step 2

    Private deal

    Each seat owns nine encrypted deck positions. The other three players publish verified decryption shares for those cards; you combine them with your local secret to read your hand.

  3. Step 3

    Bidding & tricks

    Bids and trump selection are signed public actions. Playing a card reveals your own decryption share and proof for that single position so the table can open it.

  4. Step 4

    Full-hand audit

    After the ninth trick, all 36 cards are open. Every browser replays the hand to verify card ownership, bids, and suit-following rules before recording the score.

Round messages

All four browsers validate the same signed envelopes independently.

KEY_SHARE Deck key
Each seat publishes a Ristretto255 public key share and Schnorr proof of knowledge. The combined key locks the deck; no single player knows the full secret key.
SHUFFLE Verified mix
Seats 1 through 4 shuffle and re-encrypt the 36-card deck sequentially, producing a Bayer–Groth shuffle proof verified in a WebAssembly worker.
SHARES Card deal
For each seat’s nine positions, the three non-owners publish Chaum–Pedersen proven decryption shares. Your private key stays in IndexedDB so reloads restore your hand.
ACTION Bids & plays
Records a bid, pass, diamonds call, trump choice, or card play. Card plays attach the owner’s decryption share so everyone can open that card.
AUDIT_DISCLOSE Verification
Once all nine tricks finish, each seat signs an audit marker. Every browser checks that earlier plays followed suit and awards game points toward the 12-point target.

What stays private

The public transcript contains the roster, bids, trump suit, shuffle proofs, played cards, and scores. Your unplayed cards and your private key share never leave your browser.

Local to your browser

Your unplayed cards and your secret key share in IndexedDB.

Verified by all players

Signed bids, opened cards, shuffle proofs, and end-of-hand audits.

Current limitations

All four players must stay online to advance a hand. If you reload, your browser re-verifies saved signatures and shuffle proofs before restoring your hand from local storage. Strict NATs or firewalls may block direct WebRTC links because no TURN relay is configured.

The shuffle proof implementation is experimental and has not undergone independent cryptographic audit. A disconnected or unresponsive peer can stall the table.

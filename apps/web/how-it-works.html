Behind the table

Private hands.
Shared proof.

A Tablefolk round runs between four browsers. They agree on one signed game record and verify the same encrypted deck, but each browser opens only its own unplayed cards.

The privacy boundary

What stays local. What everyone sees.

In your browser

Your signing key, your per-game decryption key, and the cards still in your hand. Shuffle choices stay inside a temporary worker.

At the table

The agreed roster and rules, encrypted deck and proofs, verified deal shares, bids, trump, played cards, and the signed action history.

After a reload, your browser re-verifies the signed history and shuffle proofs, then rebuilds your hand with the same saved local game key. No server stores a copy of that hand.

One round, step by step

No separate dealer required.

  1. 01

    Connect and agree

    An invitation brings four players together. Relays help establish the connection; signed identity checks secure direct WebRTC links. Everyone accepts the same roster and first-round rules.

  2. 02

    Lock and shuffle the deck

    Each browser contributes a game key. The 36-card deck is encrypted under their combined public key; no single browser holds the full secret key. Each player then permutes and remasks the deck, publishing a proof that the cards are still the same 36 cards without showing the new order.

  3. 03

    Deal without showing hands

    For a card assigned to you, the other three players publish verifiable decryption shares. Your browser adds its own private share to open the card locally. The other players lack that last share, so the messages normally exchanged do not open your card for them.

  4. 04

    Reveal cards as they are played

    On your turn, you sign the action and publish the missing share for the chosen card. Every browser verifies the proof, opens that card, and updates the same public trick. Your other cards remain unopened.

  5. 05

    Check the whole hand

    After all 36 cards have been played, every browser reconstructs the original hands from the verified openings and checks bids, ownership, following suit, and scoring. A hidden-hand violation can be identified at this final audit.

The central idea

One missing share keeps a card private.

Public: encrypted card + proven shares from the other three seats

Local: the owner’s secret share

Result: only the owner can read that card until they play it.

What this guarantee covers

Hidden cards, while they are hidden.

Bids, trump, turn order, played cards, and scores are public. Those clues can narrow what someone might be holding. Once a card is played, everyone knows it; after the final trick, every card is open for the audit. Private-hand rules such as following suit are checked in full at that point, since checking them earlier would require seeing the hand.

The current browser build plays one experimental round. It needs all four players connected, and its shuffle proof backend still awaits independent review. The privacy claim describes the protocol’s intended behavior under its cryptographic assumptions, not an absolute security guarantee.
